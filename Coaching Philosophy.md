# Coaching Philosophy
### Foundations for Player and Team Development

*The beliefs and values underpinning how I coach*
Prepared by Stephen Kavanagh — submitted in support of the UEFA B Licence
July 2026

*Document 1 of 3 — companion to the Game Model and Principles of Play*

---

## 1. Introduction

This document sets out the beliefs and values that underpin my coaching practice. It is the "why" and "how" behind the Game Model and Principles of Play — the two companion documents that describe what the team does on the pitch. Those documents describe playing behaviours; this one explains the coaching approach that produces them.

## 2. Methodology: Ecological Dynamics and a Constraints-Led Approach

- My coaching is grounded in ecological dynamics: learning is treated as an emergent product of the interaction between the player, the task and the environment, rather than the transmission of pre-packaged solutions.
- Practices are designed, not just delivered. I manipulate task constraints — space, numbers, rules, scoring — so the picture a player faces in training is representative of the picture they will face in a match.
- Players are given the problem, not the answer, so that decision-making is trained alongside technique rather than separated from it.

## 3. The Role of the Coach

Adopting an ecological approach changes the role of the coach — from correcting reproduced technique toward designing environments that make the right decisions likely. It does not remove coaching or explicit teaching. In the moment, this is put into practice through Dynamic Spaces — a live coaching vocabulary (Football 1/2/3) used to prompt a player's perception of their spatial responsibility rather than instructing them directly.

Where a technical principle is central to my idea of how the game should be played, I coach it directly and explicitly rather than leaving it purely to emerge. An open, "sideways-on" body shape when receiving — and receiving on the back foot to play forward or away from pressure — is one example: it is foundational to how I want the team to play, so it is taught, not just discovered.

In practice this means: design the environment first; intervene explicitly, second, when a specific technical picture needs to be seen and practised deliberately.

This is also why the player-facing version of the model (the On-Pitch Prompts / U17 Player Card, see Principles of Play §6) is deliberately thin: a small number of high-leverage prompts a player can hold and self-organise around, not a simplified copy of the full coaching detail. Fewer, well-chosen principles — not more instructions — is the same constraints-led logic applied to language rather than practice design.

## 4. Environment and Culture

- Psychological safety comes first: players need to feel safe to try, fail, and try again without the mistake becoming the story of the session.
- Errors are treated as information, not something to be avoided — they are a necessary part of how a representative learning environment works.
- Safety does not mean the absence of challenge. Practices are demanding, but failure inside them is never met with social or emotional punishment.

## 5. Purpose

Beyond results and beyond individual ability, the aim is that every player leaves with a lasting love of the game. Technical and tactical development, decision-making and results are all pursued inside that aim — not instead of it.

## 6. Priorities: Development Over Results

Where short-term results and long-term development conflict, development wins, clearly. This principle shapes selection, session design and in-match decision-making.

- Position rotation has been consistent since U7 and decreases as players mature. At U17, rotation is now narrow: where a player can best help the team has emerged from their individual abilities, so the development focus at this stage is on strengthening them within their main position rather than rotating them widely.
- Pre-match warm-ups are used as coaching opportunities, not purely physical preparation.
- Practising what most needs coaching is prioritised over "safe" selections chosen only to maximise the short-term result.

## 7. How This Connects

This philosophy underpins the Game Model — the team's playing identity — and the Principles of Play, which set out the detailed coaching points in each moment of the game. Together the three documents move from why and how (this document), to what the team is (Game Model), to the specific behaviours coached in possession, out of possession and in transition (Principles of Play).
